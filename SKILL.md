# Trend Scout

Made by Built Young (builtyoung.org). This credit is for people reading the skill. Never add it to a student's content.

Finds what a product's audience is already paying attention to, and turns it into content ideas a student founder can make this week. Built for Built Young GTM classes: an instructor runs it, each team supplies its product details, and the team leaves with trend cards to take into poster, video or build-in-public work.

## The idea to teach

Content is anything you publish that gives your audience a reason to stop scrolling: it teaches something, shows something, tells a story, or proves something. Good ideas come from three places:

1. **Your own story:** what you built, broke and learned this week. (The build-in-public skill covers this.)
2. **Questions your audience keeps asking.** These stay useful for months.
3. **Moments happening right now** that your audience already cares about.

This skill covers 2 and 3. When running a class, say this framing out loud before showing results, so students learn where ideas come from and don't just collect a list.

## Workflow

### 1. Intake (ask only for what's missing)

- Product: name, one line on what it does, the problem it solves
- Audience: who uses it (age, situation), who pays if different, and where they live
- Channels the team actually posts on (pick up to 3)
- What they can make this week: screenshots, a demo, photos, a phone camera
- Anything off-limits for the team or their school

If a team gives a vague audience ("students"), ask one narrowing question. "Grade 11 students in Ontario preparing for exams" finds real conversations; "students" finds nothing.

### 2. Search

Use whatever search and page-reading tools are available. Run both lenses.

**Questions lens (evergreen).** Search the problem in the audience's own words, not the product's words: "how do I...", "is there an app for...", "why is ... so hard", plus the places this audience talks (Reddit, forums, YouTube comments, Q&A sites, parent groups that are public). You are looking for the same question or complaint showing up more than once, and the exact wording people use.

**Moments lens (live).**
- **Calendar:** the next four weeks for this audience and region: exams, report cards, school breaks, application deadlines, holidays, seasons, sports and cultural events.
- **News:** what happened in the product's topic in the last 30 days.
- **Formats:** recurring post formats or challenges on the team's channels that sources describe as current.
- **Neighbours:** what similar products or creators in the space posted or launched recently.

For every item you keep, open the page and record the link, the date it was published, and one line on what it says. Search snippets often show the wrong date, and an old trend presented as new makes a student look out of touch.

**If there is no web access:** say so plainly. Build the calendar items from what you know and mark each "confirm the date". Then ask the team to bring three posts they each saw this week that their audience reacted to (links or screenshots) and work from those. That is a good class exercise in itself.

### 3. Filter

Drop an idea if it fails any of these:

- **Fit.** There is a real link between the moment and the problem the product solves. Test: if a stranger saw the post, would the product feel like a natural part of it, or like an ad stapled to a meme? Forced trend-jacking is the most common mistake, and audiences punish it.
- **Fresh.** Live items are dated within the last 30 days. Older ones are either evergreen questions or dropped.
- **Safe for a student's public account.** Skip tragedies, disasters and deaths, politics and elections, wars, religion, anything sexual, dangerous stunts or challenges, drama about named people, and anything that mocks a group. A post that lands badly follows a teenager around for years, and much of their audience is also under 18.
- **Rights.** Borrow the format, not the asset. No reposting someone else's video, no characters from shows or games, no music from outside the platform's own audio library.
- **Doable.** The team can make it this week with what they have.

If fewer than three ideas survive, say so and give the ones that did. Padding the list with weak ideas teaches students that any trend will do.

### 4. Write trend cards

Default: five cards per team (three live, two evergreen), ranked, best first. Use this format:

```
CARD [n] · [Live / Evergreen] · Shelf life: [this week / this month / anytime]
What's happening: [one or two plain sentences]
Source: [title] · [link] · [date published]
Why your audience cares: [one sentence, in their words where possible]
Your angle: [how the product honestly connects]
Make this: [format + channel, e.g. "problem-solution poster for Instagram", "15-second screen recording for TikTok", "founder post for LinkedIn"]
Hook: [the first line or on-screen text, 10 words or fewer]
You need: [the screenshot, clip, number or quote the team must supply]
Check before posting: [only when something applies: consent, a claim to verify, a date to confirm]
```

After the cards, add two lines: **Top pick** (which card and why) and **Skip for now** (one idea you rejected and the reason). Seeing a rejected idea teaches the filter better than a rule does.

**Example card**

```
CARD 1 · Live · Shelf life: this month
What's happening: Ontario high schools are heading into exam season, and students are posting about having too many tests in one week.
Source: [title of the page you opened] · [link] · [date]
Why your audience cares: "I have four tests in five days and no idea where to start."
Your angle: StudyFox turns a photo of your notes into a quiz, so the first step takes one minute.
Make this: problem-solution poster for Instagram, plus a 15-second screen recording of scanning one page.
Hook: Four tests. Five days. Start here.
You need: one real screenshot of the quiz screen.
Check before posting: confirm your school's exam dates.
```

(StudyFox is a fictional example. The source line must always be a page you actually opened.)

### 5. Hand off

End with the next step for the top pick:

- A poster or carousel: take the card to the ig-poster-studio skill. The "Make this" line names the layout to use.
- A founder post: take it to the build-in-public skill.
- A short video: write a three-beat script on the spot (hook, show the product, one action), 20 seconds or less.

## Honesty rules

- Never say something is trending, viral or popular unless a source you opened says so. Give that source and its date.
- Never invent view counts, statistics, quotes or user comments. If a card needs a number the team hasn't given, write `[NEEDS: ...]`.
- Quote real people's wording only from public pages, and never include a private person's username or name in a card.
- The angle must be true of the product today. If the product can't do what the trend implies, it is not a fit.

One made-up "everyone is talking about this" teaches a student that content is about faking relevance. The whole point of the class is the opposite.

## Class mode

When the instructor gives several teams at once, produce three cards per team (two live, one evergreen), in the same format, with a top pick for each. Keep commentary to one line per team so the output can be shown on screen and read aloud.

## Language

Reply in the user's language. Write each hook in the language of the channel's audience. When a team posts on both an English and a Chinese platform, write each hook natively for that platform; do not translate one into the other.
